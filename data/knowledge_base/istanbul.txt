Istanbul is a transcontinental city straddling the Bosphorus strait, with its western half in Europe and eastern half in Asia. With its long history at the centre of the Roman, Byzantine, and Ottoman empires, Istanbul offers a wealth of historic and religious monuments. The bulk of ancient sites — including Hagia Sophia, Topkapı Palace, the Blue Mosque, and the Basilica Cistern — cluster around Sultanahmet Square on the European side.

The Turkish government offers a Museum Pass for Istanbul's key museums, priced at approximately €105. The pass is valid for five days from first use and covers one entry each to Topkapı Palace and Harem, Hagia Irene, Istanbul Archaeological Museums, Istanbul Mosaic Museum, Museum for the History of Science and Technology in Islam, Museum of Turkey and Islamic Arts, Galata Mevlevi House Museum, Yıldız Palace, Rumeli Hisarı Museum, and Galata Tower. The pass also lets you skip the ticket queue at most included sites. Note that Hagia Sophia is not included and requires a separate ticket of approximately €25. Most museums in Istanbul are closed on Mondays or Wednesdays, so check schedules before visiting.

Hagia Sophia is Istanbul's most iconic monument, originally built as a cathedral in 537 CE under Emperor Justinian. It served as the world's largest cathedral for nearly a thousand years before being converted into a mosque after the Ottoman conquest in 1453. Its massive dome, Byzantine mosaics, and Islamic calligraphy panels make it a must-visit. It currently functions as a mosque; visitors should dress modestly and remove shoes before entering.

Topkapı Palace was the primary residence of the Ottoman sultans for nearly 400 years, from the 15th to the 19th century. The sprawling complex overlooks the Bosphorus and the Golden Horn, and contains courtyards, pavilions, the Imperial Treasury with the famous Topkapı Dagger and Spoonmaker's Diamond, and the Harem quarters where the sultan's family lived. Allow at least three to five hours to visit Hagia Sophia and Topkapı Palace together.

The Sultanahmet Mosque, known as the Blue Mosque for the blue İznik tiles lining its interior, sits directly across from Hagia Sophia. Built in the early 17th century, it is one of the few mosques in the world with six minarets. The mosque is free to enter but closes to tourists during prayer times. The square between the Blue Mosque and Hagia Sophia — the ancient Hippodrome — features the Obelisk of Theodosius, the Serpent Column, and the Walled Obelisk.

The Basilica Cistern is a vast underground water reservoir built by the Romans beneath what is now Sultanahmet. Supported by 336 marble columns, many recycled from ruined temples, the cistern features atmospheric lighting reflected in shallow water. Two columns rest on carved Medusa heads of mysterious origin. It is one of Istanbul's most atmospheric sites.

Galata Tower crowns the Galata district north of the Golden Horn. The medieval stone tower offers a 360-degree panoramic view of the old city, the Bosphorus, and the Asian side. It is included in the Museum Pass, though queues for entry can be long. The surrounding Galata neighbourhood has cobblestone streets, independent cafés, and vintage shops.

Istanbul Modern, located on the Karaköy waterfront near Galata, is the city's premier contemporary art museum showcasing modern Turkish art with rotating exhibitions. Nearby is the Galata Mevlevi House Museum, a former Dervish hall of the Sufi Mevlevi order — particularly interesting for those drawn to the teachings of Rumi and whirling dervish ceremonies.

The Church of the Holy Saviour in Chora (Kariye Camii) is located near the Theodosian Walls in the Edirnekapı neighbourhood. Its interior is covered with extraordinary Byzantine frescoes and mosaics dating from the 14th century, considered among the finest surviving examples of Byzantine art. The church has been converted into a mosque but remains open to visitors.

The Theodosian Walls stretch about 7 km along the western boundary of the old city peninsula, built from 408 CE to defend Constantinople. They are still remarkably intact and walking along them offers a window into antiquity. Notable points include the Yedikule Fortress (the Golden Gate) at the southern end near the Marmara coast, and restored sections near the Topkapı Gate. Some remoter sections may require caution.

Süleymaniye Mosque, built by the great architect Sinan for Sultan Suleiman the Magnificent in the 1550s, sits on a hilltop overlooking the Golden Horn. It is one of Istanbul's grandest mosques with a serene courtyard and stunning views. The complex includes Suleiman's tomb, a medrese, and a hammam.

Eyüpsultan, located up the Golden Horn, is home to the city's holiest Islamic shrine — the tomb of Abu Ayyub al-Ansari, a companion of the Prophet Muhammad. The neighbourhood has narrow cobblestone streets, traditional Ottoman atmosphere, and the Pierre Loti hilltop café with panoramic views over the Golden Horn.

Sultanahmet is the heart of historic Istanbul and the most popular area for tourists. Nearly all the major Byzantine and Ottoman monuments are within walking distance of each other. The neighbourhood is full of hotels, restaurants, and rooftop terraces with views of the Blue Mosque and the Marmara Sea.

Beyoğlu, centred on İstiklal Avenue and Taksim Square, is Istanbul's modern cultural hub. İstiklal is a famous pedestrian boulevard stretching from near the Galata Tower to Taksim Square, lined with shops, cafés, galleries, consulates, and historic buildings. A nostalgic 1920s tram rattles along its length. The surrounding neighbourhoods of Cihangir and Çukurcuma are known for antique shops and bohemian atmosphere.

Kadıköy on the Asian side is a vibrant, laid-back neighbourhood popular with locals. It has a bustling market area, independent bookshops, street art, craft beer bars, and excellent food stalls. The nearby Moda neighbourhood is known for its seaside promenade and relaxed café culture. A vintage 1960s tram runs a circular route between Kadıköy and Moda.

Üsküdar, also on the Asian side, is a more traditional and conservative neighbourhood with beautiful Ottoman mosques right on the waterfront. The view back across the Bosphorus to the old city at sunset is spectacular. The Maiden's Tower sits on a small islet just offshore between Üsküdar and Kadıköy.

The European Bosphorus shore north of Taksim is lined with pleasant neighbourhoods full of waterfront mansions (yalı) and Ottoman-era palaces including Dolmabahçe Palace, Beylerbeyi Palace, and Küçüksu Palace. Bebek, about 10 km north, is a small upscale waterfront neighbourhood with restaurants and cafés, and a great place for an evening stroll.

Across the Bosphorus on the Asian side, Çamlıca Hill is one of the highest points in the city, offering a sweeping panorama of Istanbul. It has a café and a pleasant park at its summit.

The Princes' Islands are an archipelago of nine car-free islands southeast of the city, off the Asian coast. Characterised by stunning Victorian-era wooden mansions, pine groves, and horse-drawn carriages, they offer a peaceful escape from the city. Büyükada is the largest and most visited island.

Istanbul is famous for its cats. Known affectionately as "Catstanbul," the city is home to anywhere from 125,000 to over a million feline residents, mostly tabbies. They are generally friendly, well-fed, and healthy. Locals consider them communally owned rather than stray, and you will see them lounging in mosques, shops, and cafés throughout the city.

Tulips have a deep historical connection to Istanbul. The flower was first introduced to the Netherlands from Istanbul during the 18th-century Ottoman Tulip Era. After falling out of fashion, tulips made a major comeback in the 21st century and now serve as a symbol of the city. They bloom from late March to early May, with April being the peak season. The best places to see large tulip displays are Sultanahmet Park, Gülhane Park, Emirgan Park on the European Bosphorus, and Çamlıca Hill on the Asian side.

Turkish hamams (bathhouses) are a quintessential Istanbul cultural experience. Sultanahmet has many historical hamams, some very extravagant and catering mainly to tourists. A traditional hamam visit typically includes a steam room session, a vigorous scrub on a heated marble slab, and a foam massage. Historic hamams like Çemberlitaş Hamamı and Ayasofya Hürrem Sultan Hamamı are popular choices.

The classic Bosphorus cruise departs from Eminönü and travels north along the strait toward the Black Sea, passing Ottoman palaces, waterfront mansions, fortresses, and fishing villages on both the European and Asian shores. The full-length public ferry cruise goes to Anadolu Kavağı near the northern entrance of the Bosphorus, where you can visit the Yoros Castle ruins overlooking the Black Sea. The cruise takes most of a day and is very popular, so arrive early. There are also shorter private cruise options available.

The Grand Bazaar (Kapalı Çarşı) is one of the oldest and largest covered markets in the world, with over 4,000 shops spread across a labyrinth of vaulted streets. It sells carpets, ceramics, jewellery, leather goods, textiles, and souvenirs. Bargaining is expected and part of the experience. The bazaar sits on what was once the western terminus of the Silk Road.

The Spice Bazaar (Mısır Çarşısı), also known as the Egyptian Bazaar, is located near Eminönü. It is famous for its colourful displays of spices, dried fruits, nuts, Turkish delight, teas, and other delicacies. Prices in the Spice Bazaar tend to be inflated for tourists — better deals on the same products can be found elsewhere in the city.

Rüstem Pasha Mosque, located on a raised platform near the Spice Bazaar, is a small but exquisite mosque designed by architect Sinan. Its interior is entirely covered in fine İznik tiles with intricate floral patterns. It is often overlooked by tourists but considered one of the finest examples of Ottoman tile work.

Istanbul's cuisine is a highlight of any visit. Esnaf lokantası restaurants are traditional canteen-style eateries serving a variety of classic Turkish dishes from soups to stews to desserts at reasonable prices and are the best way to experience authentic local food.

Balık-Ekmek (fish and bread) is an iconic Istanbul street food — freshly grilled mackerel served in a crusty bread roll with lettuce, onions, and a squeeze of lemon, traditionally eaten by the waterfront at Eminönü.

Döner is a beloved Turkish dish of seasoned meat cooked on a vertical rotisserie, served wrapped in flatbread (dürüm), in a sandwich, or plated with rice and salad. It is a staple of Turkish street food.

Kokoreç is a traditional Turkish street food made from seasoned lamb intestines wrapped around sweetbreads and grilled over charcoal, then chopped with spices and served in bread. It is a flavourful local favourite.

Kumpir is a loaded baked potato filled with cheese, butter, and an array of toppings including corn, pickles, sausage, and Russian salad. The best kumpir in Istanbul is found at the waterfront stalls in Ortaköy.

Lahmacun, sometimes called Turkish pizza, is a thin crispy flatbread topped with spiced minced meat, herbs, and onions, typically rolled with lemon juice and fresh vegetables. Pide is a boat-shaped flatbread with various fillings such as cheese, minced meat, or eggs.

Simit is a sesame-crusted circular bread similar to a bagel but lighter and crunchier, sold everywhere from street carts and enjoyed with tea, cheese, or jam. It is a staple of Turkish breakfast culture.

Turkish Delight (lokum) is a classic souvenir purchase. It is best bought fresh rather than pre-packaged, and in a variety of flavours — pistachio is particularly popular. İstiklal Avenue has several quality confectionery shops selling lokum and other Turkish sweets by weight.

Turkish tea (çay) is the national drink, brewed strong in a double teapot and served in small tulip-shaped glasses with cube sugar. It is offered virtually everywhere — in shops, offices, and on the street — and is one of the small luxuries of daily life in Turkey.

Turkish coffee is made by boiling finely ground coffee beans in a small pot (cezve), usually with sugar, and serving it in a small cup where the grounds settle. It is an important part of Turkish culture, and some people read fortunes from the remaining coffee grounds.

Freshly squeezed fruit juices — orange, pomegranate, kiwi, and various blends — are sold from stands throughout the city and are a refreshing treat, especially in warmer months.

Roasted chestnuts are sold from carts around the city and make a warming winter snack. Roasted corn on the cob is also popular from street carts year-round.

Istanbul has a vibrant nightlife scene spread across several neighbourhoods. Beyoğlu has lively bars and cafés with live music. Kadıköy has a compact, easy-going nightlife with local pubs and wine houses. Karaköy is known for underground parties with views over the Marmara Sea. Nişantaşı attracts young entrepreneurs and artists, with higher prices than Taksim. Bebek on the European Bosphorus is a great spot for a waterfront dinner followed by bar-hopping. Nightclubs are found all over the city, with the most popular ones in Pera and Ortaköy.

Meyhanes are traditional Turkish taverns originating in Ottoman times, serving rakı (anise-flavoured spirit) alongside meze (small dishes). They come in both traditional and modern styles, some with live music or belly dancing. Seaside meyhanes along the Bosphorus are particularly atmospheric.

Nargile (water pipe) smoking is a popular social activity in Istanbul. Some of the best nargile cafés are along Yeniçeriler Caddesi near the Grand Bazaar, in secluded courtyards like Çorlulu Ali Paşa Medresesi. In Beyoğlu, Ortakahve offers a wide range of flavours.

Istanbul has accommodation for every budget. Sultanahmet has quality mid-range hotels, many with rooftop terraces overlooking the Blue Mosque or the Marmara Sea, as well as hostels popular with independent travellers. Taksim and the surrounding area offer moderate hotels for budget travellers and is convenient for nightlife and shopping. Harbiye, close to Taksim and Nişantaşı, has a range of international-standard hotels and apartments. Upscale hotels can be found along the European Bosphorus shore and in the western suburbs. Bağdat Avenue on the Asian side is a long boulevard with good restaurants, boutiques, and high-end shops.

For shopping, Istanbul's Ottoman bazaars carry carpets, kilims, ceramics, jewellery, leather goods, and textiles. Rugs and kilims can be a good buy but bargaining is essential — most rug shops in the tourist areas are aimed at the tourist trade. Istanbul is also known for exceptional jewellery craftsmanship from both Armenian and Turkish artisans, including chalcedony, a semi-precious gemstone named after the ancient town of Chalcedon across the Bosphorus.

Nuts and spices are a popular purchase and can be found in abundance at the Spice Bazaar and other central markets like those in Üsküdar.

Modern shopping malls are found throughout the city, especially in the eastern and western suburbs. Textile wholesale is concentrated in Laleli, while upscale fashion retail is in Nişantaşı on the European side and along Bağdat Avenue on the Asian side.

Istanbul has a warm Mediterranean-influenced climate. Summers (June to September) are hot and humid with temperatures around 28–33°C. Winters (December to February) are cool and rainy, sometimes with snow, and temperatures around 3–8°C. Spring (April to May) and autumn (September to November) are the most pleasant times to visit, with mild temperatures and fewer crowds. April is especially beautiful for the tulip displays.

A recommended walking tour of the old city could start with Hagia Sophia and Topkapı Palace (allow at least three to five hours for both), then continue to the Blue Mosque and the Hippodrome square with its ancient obelisks. From there, visit the Museum of Turkish and Islamic Arts nearby, then descend to the Little Hagia Sophia mosque with its quiet garden. Head uphill to the Sokollu Mehmet Pasha Mosque, known for its superb İznik tiles. This route covers the essential Sultanahmet sights in a single day.

Another walking route heads from Sultanahmet to Eminönü, where you can visit the New Mosque, explore the Spice Bazaar, and find the Rüstem Pasha Mosque with its excellent tiles on a raised platform near the old clothes market. From Eminönü, a trip up the Golden Horn to Eyüpsultan is worthwhile — visit the mosque complex and shrine, enjoy the traditional atmosphere, then walk back along the Golden Horn to see the Kariye Mosque (Church of the Holy Saviour in Chora), the Yavuz Selim Mosque with its view of the Golden Horn, the Fatih Mosque, the Şehzade Mosque, and finally the Süleymaniye Mosque.

The Galata Bridge, spanning the Golden Horn between Eminönü and Karaköy, is a landmark in its own right. The upper level carries traffic and pedestrians, while the lower level is lined with fish restaurants. Fishermen line the railings at all hours, and the bridge offers beautiful views of the old city skyline, especially at sunset.

Ortaköy, on the European Bosphorus shore beneath the first Bosphorus Bridge, is a lively waterfront neighbourhood known for its photogenic baroque mosque sitting right at the water's edge. The weekend craft market, waterside cafés, and kumpir stalls make it a popular destination for both locals and visitors.

The Istanbul Archaeological Museums, located between Topkapı Palace and Gülhane Park, comprise three museums in one complex: the Archaeological Museum with Greco-Roman antiquities including the Alexander Sarcophagus, the Museum of the Ancient Orient with Mesopotamian artefacts, and the Tiled Kiosk Museum with Ottoman ceramics. It is one of the most important archaeological collections in the world.

Gülhane Park, adjacent to Topkapı Palace, is one of Istanbul's oldest public parks. Once part of the palace grounds, it offers shaded walkways, flower gardens, and views over the Bosphorus and the Golden Horn. It is an especially pleasant spot during the spring tulip season.

Dolmabahçe Palace, on the European Bosphorus shore between Beşiktaş and Kabataş, was built in the mid-19th century as a more European-style replacement for Topkapı Palace. Its opulent interior features crystal chandeliers, gilded ceilings, and the world's largest Bohemian crystal chandelier weighing 4.5 tonnes. Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, founder of the Turkish Republic, spent his last days here.

The New Istanbul business district north of Taksim features most of the city's skyscrapers around Levent and Maslak, presenting a totally different skyline from the old city. The southern reaches of this area around Osmanbey, Kurtuluş, and Nişantaşı have fine neo-classical and Art Nouveau buildings from the turn of the 20th century, worth a stroll for architecture enthusiasts.

Islak Hamburger (wet burger) is a distinctive Turkish street food — a soft bun with a meat patty drenched in a garlicky tomato sauce and steamed until moist and flavourful. It is a late-night snack staple, particularly popular around Taksim. Patso is another quick Turkish snack: a sandwich stuffed with French fries and topped with ketchup and mayonnaise.

Miniaturk, located in Sütlüce on the Golden Horn, is a miniature park featuring scale models of landmarks from across the former Ottoman Empire. It is a fun and educational stop, especially for families.

The Military Museum in the Harbiye district north of Taksim Square hosts Ottoman military music concerts (Mehter) every afternoon. The museum displays weapons, armour, and military artefacts from the Ottoman period.

Nearby destinations from Istanbul include Bursa, the first capital of the Ottoman Empire, known for its grand mosques, thermal baths, and the ski resort on Mount Uludağ. The ancient city of Troy and the Gallipoli battlefields on the Dardanelles are accessible as day trips or overnight excursions. Edirne, near the Bulgarian border, has the magnificent Selimiye Mosque, another masterwork of architect Sinan. The Princes' Islands offer a quick escape by ferry for a peaceful day trip.

The Golden Horn ferry from Eminönü or Karaköy to Eyüpsultan offers a scenic and affordable way to see the historic waterway, passing through neighbourhoods like Balat, Fener, and Cibali along the way. Balat and Fener are colourful, historic neighbourhoods on the European shore of the Golden Horn, known for their painted houses, Greek and Jewish heritage, and increasingly trendy café scene.
