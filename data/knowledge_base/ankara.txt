Ankara is the capital of Turkey, centrally located on the plateau of Central Anatolia. It is a sprawling modern city built around an ancient citadel, with a population of almost 5.8 million (second in size to Istanbul). Although many tourists transit through Ankara on the way to Cappadocia, the city has a rich heritage in its museums, galleries, and mosques that make it well worth a longer visit.

Until 1923, Ankara was a provincial town of fewer than 30,000 people. Then Atatürk made it the capital of the new Republic of Turkey, a deliberate shift from the Ottoman capital of Istanbul with its Byzantine political life. The plateau was swiftly covered by concrete buildings and highways, leavened by parks and woodlands and occasional striking architecture. The city was already ancient when the Greeks called it "the anchor" (Αγκυρα). Hattian, Hittite, Lydian, Phrygian, and Galatian civilizations had risen and fallen before them; Persians, Romans, and Byzantines were yet to come. In 1402 Timur stormed all the way from Kabul and won a battle that almost extinguished the Ottoman dynasty. For the next 500 years Ankara was an Ottoman city and province, giving the name "Angora" to long-haired cats, goats, rabbits, and mohair textiles. Atatürk rallied resistance in Ankara during the War of Independence, and the city's unconquered status made it a fitting capital.

Ankara's neighborhoods each have their own character. The city center is the broad strip from Ulus south through Sıhhiye to Kızılay. Ulus means "nation," as the republic's original parliament and major institutions were here, grouped around an equestrian statue of Atatürk. Sıhhiye sits on either side of the railway tracks. Kızılay was named for the headquarters of the Red Crescent and serves as the main commercial and transit hub. The Citadel area east of Ulus includes Hisar around the hilltop castle and Hamamönü just south down the hill, home to restored Ottoman architecture. Further south from Kızılay are the upmarket districts of Kavaklıdere, Gaziosmanpaşa, and Çankaya, where the city's most expensive hotels, restaurants, and embassies are found.

Ankara has a semi-arid continental climate with hot, dry summers and cold, snowy winters. Most rainfall comes in spring, with afternoon showers and thunderstorms. Summers regularly top 30°C in July and August, but humidity is low, and nights can be chilly when a breeze starts up. Early autumn offers the best weather, with cold nights but mild, dry daytimes. Winters are cold and often snowy, though snow is seldom heavy and extreme cold below -20°C is very rare.

Ankara Castle is a volcanic outcrop east of Ulus that has likely been fortified since antiquity, with the earliest traces from the Phrygians of the 8th century BC. The castle was reinforced by the Galatians and then the Romans. Visitors stroll up through cobbled streets to the double walls and can climb one of the towers for panoramic views of the city and distant mountains. Free admission, open daily 10:00–20:00. The nearby North Tower is another bastion of the citadel, marked by a fluttering Turkish flag. The Sultan Alaeddin Mosque, 100 m west of the castle, is the city's oldest mosque, built in 1178 AD according to the inscription on its walnut mimber. Below the castle, the Turk-Russian Friendship House at Kale Kapısı Sk 20 is a bright modern art gallery with three floors of art including Belarusian and Ukrainian sections, open daily 10:00–17:00, about 20 TL entry.

The Museum of Anatolian Civilizations at Gözcü Sk 2 is one of Ankara's must-visit attractions, with an excellent display ranging from prehistory to the proto-Greek and Roman eras. Adult admission is 80 TL, open daily 08:30–17:30. Across the street, the small private Kelime Museum at Gözcü Sk 11 displays art (Tu–Su 10:00–17:00, Turkish signage only). Nearby, the Koç Museum at Depo Sk 1 is housed in Çengel Han, a caravanserai built in 1523, and is packed with exhibits on transport, communication, and science since the 1850s. Adult admission 625 TL, open Tu–Th 10:00–17:00 and Sa–Su 10:00–19:00.

The Arslanhane Mosque (Ahi Şerafeddin Camii) on Can Sk was built in 1290 in Seljuk style. It is well worth the hike up the hill for its finely carved interior, with a ceiling supported by a forest of wooden columns. In 2023, it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as one of five wooden mosques in Central Anatolia. Nearby, the Ahielvan Mosque on Koyunpazarı Sk, built in the late 14th and early 15th centuries, has a richly carved walnut mimber. The Roman Theatre on Bentderesi Cd preserves remains of the stage and backstage area; free to enter, M–Sa 09:00–19:00, Su 10:00–17:00.

The Hacı Bayram Mosque at Sarıbağ Sk 13 was built in 1428, restored by Mimar Sinan in the 16th century, and decorated with Kütahya tiles in the 18th century. Hacı Bayram Veli (1352–1430) was a poet and Sufi teacher, and his ornate tomb is a pilgrimage site. Open 24 hours. Right next to it stands the Temple of Augustus (Monumentum Ancyranum), built from 25 BC over an older Phrygian temple for the worship of Emperor Augustus. In the 5th century it was converted into a church by the Byzantines. Only the side walls and ornate doorway remain. Free admission.

The Roman Baths at Çankırı Cd 43 are ruins of a large bath complex built under Emperor Caracalla circa 200 AD and in use for 500 years. They follow the classical design of frigidarium (cold room), tepidarium (warm room), and caldarium (hot room). Adult 20 TL, open daily 10:00–16:30. The Column of Julianus (Julian Sütunu) is a 15-meter column erected in 362 CE to commemorate the visit of Emperor Julian, the last pagan Roman emperor, who stopped in Ankara while marching against Persia.

In Ulus, the Victory Monument (Zafer Anıtı) in Ulus Square is a marble and bronze monument with Atatürk on horseback, erected in 1927. Since it predates the 1928 alphabet reform, it is one of the few republican monuments inscribed in Ottoman Turkish using Arabic script. The Museum of the War of Independence at Cumhuriyet Cd 14/22 occupies the first site of the Turkish Republican Parliament (1920–1924) and displays photos and memorabilia from the War of Independence (daily 09:00–17:00, Turkish signage). The Cardo Maximus nearby is a stretch of Roman road 216 m long, discovered in 1995.

Anıtkabir is an extensive hilltop site centered on the mausoleum of Mustafa Kemal Atatürk (1881–1938), founder of the Republic of Turkey. The site is free to enter 24 hours. The approach includes the 262-meter "Road of Lions" flanked by statues representing ancient Turkic tribes, leading to the Ceremonial Plaza where flagstone patterns echo traditional rugs and kilims. The Hall of Honor holds the symbolic sarcophagus; Atatürk's actual resting place is 7 m below. The Atatürk and Independence War Museum beneath the Hall displays belongings and memorabilia of Atatürk, the 1915 Gallipoli battles, and the War of Independence (M–Sa 09:00–17:00, free). The Mausoleum of İsmet İnönü (1884–1973), Atatürk's right-hand man and the country's second president, stands on the southwest side of the Ceremonial Plaza.

The Kocatepe Mosque at Dr Mediha Eldem Sk 67, one kilometer southeast of Kızılay, is a neoclassical Ottoman structure built from 1967 to 1987. Free, open 24 hours. The Cenâbî Ahmet Paşa Mosque at Ulucanlar Cd 56 was built by the famous architect Sinan in 1566 in finely crafted Ankara stone, with a white marble mimber and mihrap. The Molla Great Mosque (Molla Büyük Cami) on Yasa Sk is a modest building constructed around 1500.

The Çankaya Mansion was Atatürk's presidential home; "Çankaya" came to mean the presidency, similar to "White House" or "Kremlin." In 2018 the president's residence moved to the Ak Palace, but the mansion and government campus remain. The Atakule Tower is a 125-meter telecoms tower with a rotating observation deck and restaurant in the Çankaya district. Botanical Park, the large green space north of the tower bounded by Cinnah Cd and Çankaya Cd, is free and open 24 hours. The Hittite Sun Disc Statue marks the entrance to Sıhhiye beneath the railway tracks, its deer, oxen, and sun disc commemorating the Anatolian Hittite civilization that dominated from the 18th to 12th century BC.

The Ulucanlar Prison Museum at Ulucanlar Cd 63 was a barracks converted to a prison in 1925 that held almost 800 inmates. It was a notorious instrument of political repression; 19 prisoners were hanged, mostly for being Marxist. It closed in 2006 and became a museum in 2011. Adult 60 TL, Tu–Su 10:00–16:00. The Chess Museum at Basamaklı Sk 3 houses more ancient and modern chess sets than you'd think possible, including unusual variants like the three-sided game. Adult 15 TL, Tu–Su 10:00–18:00.

The Painting and Sculpture Museum at Türkocağı Sk hosts Turkish art from the late 1800s to present day (Tu–Su 09:00–18:00). Behind it, the Ethnography Museum at Türkocağı Cd 4 displays a huge array of Turkic art covering painting, porcelain, woodwork, clothing, carpets, metalwork, and weaponry. Built 1925–28, it housed the sarcophagus of Atatürk from 1938 to 1953 pending completion of Anıtkabir (daily 08:30–17:30). CerModern at Altınsoy Cd 3 is a contemporary art museum in the former railway power plant, adult 40 TL (Tu–Su 10:00–19:00). The Hacettepe Art Museum on the university campus has an extensive display of Turkish painters from the early Republic to present (M–F 10:00–16:00). The Vakıf Museum at Atatürk Blv 23 near Ulus has an impressive collection of antique Turkish carpets, candle holders, Korans, woodworks, and tiles in a 1927 building (Tu–Su 09:00–17:00, free).

The Ankara Opera and Ballet at Atatürk Blv 20, opened in 1948, hosts theatre, opera, and ballet performances. The city is also home to the Presidential Symphony Orchestra and the Atatürk Culture Center. Annual cultural events include the Ankara International Music Festival in April, the Bilkent Mayfest, a Jazz Festival in October/November, the Ankara Film Festival in November, and the Şefika Kutluer Festival of music in December.

Gençlik Park stretches near the center with a large ornamental lake and open-air events. Seğmenler Park, Dikmen Valley, and Abdi İpekçi Park near Sıhhiye offer green space. Göksu Park in Etimesgut is set around Susuz Lake with an island restaurant, cafes, and funfair (daily 06:00–23:00). Outside the center, Atatürk Forest Farm is an extensive model farm established by Atatürk to prove the steppes around Ankara could become productive farmland; its barn serves as a museum of farming methods (W–Su 09:00–17:00, free). Lake Mogan is a 5-kilometer recreational lake 20 km south with camping, fishing, picnic spots, and walking trails. The Altınköy Open Air Museum reconstructs 1930s–50s Anatolian village life with relocated buildings and craft demonstrations (Tu–Su 10:00–20:00, adult 10 TL). Tulumtaş Cave near Gölbaşı is a prehistoric cave discovered during road construction. The Golden Pavilion Museum (Merik Konağı) on İhsan Doğramacı Blv is an eccentric reproduction of an Ottoman-style wooden mansion built in the 1960s, now open as a museum with a cafe inside (Tu–Su 10:30–16:30, 250 TL).

The approaches to the Citadel along Kale Kapısı Sk have been a merchandising area for centuries, with outlets for carpets, leather, and antiques. The Ulus bazaar area has rows of small food outlets where gözleme, simit, and similar snacks are available cheaply. Major shopping malls include Ankamall at Mevlana Blv 2, Armada at Dumlupınar Blv 6, and Karum AVM at Iran Cd 21 next to the Sheraton, all open daily 10:00–22:00. Kentpark AVM near ODTÜ is a large mall with eateries and pubs popular with university students.

Ankara has a variety of local and international dining, including Asian, French, Italian, Persian, and Somalian cuisine. For budget meals, Kızılay has a pedestrianized block east of Atatürk Blv full of little cafes and takeaways. Çiğ köfte and köfte carts are found all over the city. Ward Restaurant on Bayındır-1 Sk in Kızılay specializes in Iraqi food (daily 08:00–00:00). Kırık Oklava at Cemal Gürsel Cd 16 near Ankara University serves inexpensive wraps and pancakes (daily 08:30–22:30). The area around the National Library has many student-friendly cafes and chain fast food.

Mid-range restaurants include Tarihi Çiçek Lokantası at Necatibey Cd 15 (daily 08:00–21:00), Cafe des Cafes at Tunalı Hilmi Cd 83/A in Kavaklıdere with Italian and Spanish choices, and Kınacızade Konağı at Kale Kapısı Sk 28 near the castle—atmospheric and good value (daily 10:30–21:00). Uludağ Kebapçısı at Denizciler Cd 54 is an above-par kebab restaurant (daily 11:00–22:00). Hanzade Konağı in Hamamönü is a stylish restaurant with plants hanging from walls and ceiling. Urumchi Uyghur Restaurant at Ziya Gökalp Cd 76 offers a great range of Asian cuisines (daily 11:00–09:30). Tarihi Mutfak Lokantası at Mithatpaşa Cd 64 is known for making the best beyti kebab in Ankara. Sudem at Konur Sk 63 is a tavern with tapas and meze-style servings and Turkish music (daily 11:00–01:00). The Aşkabat Cd strip west of Anıtkabir and Esat Cd are both known for their rows of restaurants.

For upscale dining, the Gaziosmanpaşa area of Çankaya has establishments such as Trilye Restaurant, Naya Restaurant Lounge at Atakule, Develi1912 on Arjantin Cd, and Louise Cafe Brasserie on Filistin Cd. Quick China at Uğur Mumcu Sk 64/B serves excellent Chinese, Korean, Japanese, and Thai cuisine (daily 11:00–22:00).

Most cafes and restaurants serve beer, wine, and rakı. Ankara's main nightlife strips are around Tunalı Hilmi Cd and Sakarya Sk, with Bahçeli as a third option. Meyhanes are taverns offering fixed meze-style menus with drinks and traditional Turkish music and dancing. Notable bars include Soul Pub at Olgunlar Cd 18 with generous pub food, Corvus at Bestekar Cd 59 for lively rock music (daily 17:00–02:00), Hayyami wine bar at Bestekar Cd 82B, and Sixtiees Pub at Tunalı Hilmi Cd 96. Afitap on Tunus Cd is a well-known meyhane. Clubs include IF Performance Hall and 6:45 on Tunus Cd, and Kite on Güvenlik Cd.

Budget accommodation includes Bihostel at Ataç-2 Sk 46 in Kızılay, a friendly well-equipped hostel with dorms and private rooms open all year. Mid-range options include Yeni Bahar Oteli at Çankırı Cd 25 north of Ulus Square (clean and helpful), Divan Ankara Çukurhan on Depo Sk near the castle (a charming hotel in a former marketplace), Ankara Gold at Güfte Cd 4 off Tunus Cd (clean, friendly, central), Hotel Midas at Tunus Cd 20 in Kavaklıdere, King Hotel at Güvenlik Cd 13 (simple rooms but good value near Parliament), Ramada at Tunalı Hilmi Cd 66, and Radisson Blu at Istiklal Cd 20 near Ulus. For splurge accommodation, the Sheraton Ankara on Şehit Ömer Haluk Sipahioğlu Sk is well-reviewed for comfort and service, the Ankara HiltonSA at Tahran Cd 12 is efficient and friendly, the Crowne Plaza at 171st Sk is a smart business hotel, and the Mövenpick Ankara at Yaşam Cd 1 is a slick business hotel near the bus station.

Locals are generally friendly and helpful, and university students are keen to practice their English. Service staff may have limited English compared to Western capitals, so a translation app is useful. Ankara is welcoming to visitors and offers a depth of historical and cultural experience that rewards those who take time to explore beyond the transit stop.
